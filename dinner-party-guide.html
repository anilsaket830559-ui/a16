The Hosting Guide

Menus one person can serve, what to make ahead, and how to stay at the table.

The difference between a good evening and an exhausting one is decided days before anyone arrives, in the choice of menu. Almost every hosting disaster is really a planning decision made optimistically.

Choose a menu you can serve alone

The rule is one dish that needs attention at the last minute, and no more. A braise, a roast or a tray bake can sit; a risotto, a soufflé and anything fried cannot. If two courses both need the hob at seven o’clock, change one of them now rather than discovering it later.

What to make ahead

WhenWhat
Two days beforeStock, dressings, pickles, anything braised (it improves)
The day beforePudding, dips, marinades, peeled potatoes in water
The morningVegetable prep, table laid, drinks chilled, plates out
On the dayThe roast, the sides, the sauce

Work out quantities honestly

Under-catering makes people uncomfortable and over-catering makes you tired and wasteful. The Portion Planner gives per-person figures for each course. Add a little to the cheap and filling things, keep the expensive ones exact, and pick one dish that reheats well so the leftovers have a purpose.

Write the timeline down

Not in your head: on paper, stuck to a cupboard. Start from the time you want to sit down and work backwards. Our Dinner Timeline will draft it for you, and the discipline of looking at it every twenty minutes is what stops the greens being forgotten.

Build in twenty minutes of slack. Something always takes longer than it should.

Laying a table

Cutlery works from the outside in. Water glass above the knife, and any second glass to its right. Keep the centre low enough to see over; tall arrangements kill conversation across the table. Candles should be lit before people arrive, not fussed with during the starter.

Pacing the evening

Something to eat within ten minutes of arrival settles everybody. Leave a genuine gap between courses; thirty minutes between main and pudding is not a failure of service, it is how an evening breathes. Clear plates when most people have finished, not the instant the first person puts down a fork.

When something goes wrong

It will. The roast is underdone: carve it and finish the slices in a hot pan or back in the oven for five minutes. Something is burnt: cut the burnt part away and say nothing. You are running forty minutes late: put out bread, olives and anything else edible and let people enjoy themselves. Nobody minds a delay they are not made anxious about.

Allergies and what people eat

Ask when you invite, not on the night, and take the answer seriously: allergies are a medical matter, not a preference. Keep the packaging of anything processed so you can check it, and keep one serving spoon per dish. If someone has a serious allergy, cook their portion first on clean surfaces and tell them honestly what is in everything.

The only rule that matters

Sit down. A simple dinner eaten together beats an ambitious one served by somebody who spent the evening alone in the kitchen.
